Chapter 2.2

The Pockels effect

laygradexpert in "Go deeper"last verified 2026-10-254 references

Light slows down inside a crystal. In lithium niobate, how much it slows down depends on the voltage you apply. Change the voltage, and you change the light.

Split a beam into two paths, run one of them past a pair of electrodes, and bring the paths back together. When the two beams arrive in step, they add up and the light comes out. Shift one by half a wavelength and they cancel: the light goes dark. That switch is a Mach-Zehnder modulator, and lithium niobate versions of it have carried fiber-optic traffic for decades.

Half-wave voltage Vπ = 2.0 V template (illustrative; real devices: see the Modulator Designer).

At 0.00 V, 100% of the light comes out. The crystal did nothing but change its refractive index by a few parts per million. No moving parts, no free electrons to push around: the response is effectively instantaneous, which is why these modulators reach well beyond 100 GHz.

Why this crystal

Many crystals show the effect. Lithium niobate combines a large electro-optic coefficient with transparency from the visible to the mid-infrared, a mature growth industry, and, since 2017, thin films that squeeze light into waveguides a fraction of a micrometer tall [1][2].

Its biggest coefficient, r33, works when the field and the light's polarization both lie along the crystal's polar c axis. That single fact decides how every modulator is cut and laid out. The x-cut wafer, with c in the plane, is the standard choice.
